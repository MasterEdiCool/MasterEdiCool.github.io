GE 1502 Final Project — Design Notebook

Engineering design notebook for our Cornerstone 2 final project: a hands-on STEM electronics learning toy for 5th graders at Melrose Leadership Academy in Oakland. Latest entries at the top.

Team: Edison Chen, Samuel Zhao, Minyoung Sung

  1. April 10, 2026 Documentation

    M7 documentation and showcase debrief

    Final M7 writeup. Circuit Blocks is a puzzle game where students complete a circuit by placing and reorienting component and wire boxes, with tactile feedback — LEDs, buzzers, motors — when the circuit closes. The functional elements section covered the component lineup (potentiometer, buzzer, motor, LED, button, battery) and how they connect through corner, straight, and three-way wire boxes to teach simple circuits, polarity, and parallel connections. Captured what we heard from the 5th graders too: they responded strongly to the tactile feedback, and the main thing they flagged was that the boxes should be looser and easier to slot in and pull out. Photo log of the final prototype is done.

    M7 final documentation set
  2. April 8, 2026 Final

    Wiring diagrams, bilingual instructions, feedback planning

    Two-hour wrap-up session in the makerspace. We drew the wire paths on the outside of each box so kids can visually trace how the circuit closes, then made bilingual instruction cards in Canva — English and a direct Spanish translation — and printed them. Filled in the feedback planning matrix with five project goals (interest in STEM, engagement, safety, durability, bilingual usability), each with a metric, a data-collection method, and the reasoning behind it. From the showcase: kids loved the concept and were excited to step through as many levels as they could; the Spanish instructions were genuinely useful for the Spanish-dominant students; nothing electrical broke under rough handling. Honest miss: we underestimated how hard the kids would yank on the handles and a few snapped. The component and wire boxes themselves came through intact.

    Final prototype + bilingual instruction cards
  3. April 3, 2026 Iteration

    Handle redesign without reprinting every box

    Spent a long time iterating on a handle design that wouldn't require reprinting all the boxes. Ideas we ruled out: full reprint (too much wasted filament), drilling holes for a string handle, screwing in nails (visually broken on the component boxes where the component already pokes out the top). The winning idea, settled on the spot: print a very thin lid layer with the handle integrated into it, glue that lid onto the existing box, and sand the seam so it blends in naturally. That fixes the glue surface-area problem — a tiny handle glued directly to a box would shear off, but a full lid spreads the load. Also went over every box with more copper tape so the connection is reliable every time.

    Lid-with-handle print on top of existing boxes
  4. March 31, 2026 Testing

    Casing fit-check and copper-tape conductivity test

    Today's goal was to check the printed casing against our boxes and verify connections end-to-end. Most boxes fit, but a few were slightly off — even with identical print settings, the dimensions drifted between prints — so we sanded them flush. Stuck copper tape on the casing and on a few components to test conductivity, and the connections held. The real problem that surfaced was usability, not electrical: without handles, the boxes were awkward to pull out cleanly. That goes to the top of next session's list.

    Casing with copper-tape grid
  5. March 27, 2026 Iteration

    First casing print + reworking the existing boxes

    Picked up the first print of the casing and tried to fit components — the tolerances were off, so we re-toleranced and reprinted. Bigger issue: the existing component boxes weren't designed to slide into a grid casing. We walked through each design and settled on a uniform fix — remove the bottom lid (still gives us access to the inside) and resize so the boxes slide into the grid cells. For the component boxes where the component pokes out the top, we'll just glue the component into place so it can't shift while a kid is handling the box.

  6. March 25, 2026 Ideation

    New conductivity approach: copper-tape grid

    Each of us came in with an independent idea for fixing the inconsistent washer-to-washer connections. The concept we landed on: run copper tape across the casing in a grid, and run more copper tape through the inside of each box, so when a box drops onto its cell the tape contacts close the circuit — no more relying on washers to butt up tightly against each other. We dropped the washer plan entirely. I started the SolidWorks model for the new casing.

  7. March 25, 2026 PM Handoff

    M5 planning and scheduling

    Hour-long planning session — when2meet to find days the three of us are free (Tuesdays and Fridays are cleanest), and a quick review of what's left to take the prototype from "works sometimes" to a finished product. The recurring failure mode is still the connection between boxes. Action item for next session: each person brings a concrete proposal.

  8. March 21, 2026 Debugging

    Diagnosing the connection problem (and a fix)

    Three-hour session focused on why our boxes were only conducting sometimes. After testing different configurations, we identified the failure points and developed a more reliable scheme — increase the conductive surface area by laying down more copper tape around the washers, so even a slightly imperfect fit still completes the circuit. We also finished soldering all the major component boxes (button, buzzer, battery, and so on) and a big chunk of the remaining wire boxes. Finalized the SolidWorks lid designs but held off mass-printing them until after the aesthetics milestone.

    Soldered component boxes on the bench
  9. March 18, 2026 Build

    Frame rebuild and a new connection bug

    Two-hour session. Laser-cut a tighter version of the 5x5 board frame and super-glued the edges (the previous version's hot glue was failing). Soldered 7 of the 19 boxes that need wiring. Hit a new problem during testing: even when two soldered washers were touching, the LED would only light up sometimes — same configuration, different result. We didn't solve it tonight; it's the top priority for the next session.

    Black-PLA boxes mid-assembly
  10. March 16, 2026 Build

    Soldering pass and SolidWorks drawings

    Long session focused on soldering wires inside each component box and bonding them to the washers with copper tape. Frustrating work — the same solder + tape technique would work on one box and not the next, so we redid several from scratch. We also finished SolidWorks drawings for the motor, button, LED, buzzer, and battery blocks plus the 2x1 connector, and assigned distinct colors so the boxes are easy to tell apart at a glance. Sam floated an alternate connection idea — fit the wire between the washer and the printed hole instead of relying on the washer face — worth testing next session.

    SolidWorks drawings — component blocks
  11. March 13, 2026 CAD

    Dimensions, tolerances, and printing

    About 1.5 hours of design plus another ~2 hours of print time. We remeasured everything because the earlier prints had drifted out of spec, then used those dimensions to model all the component boxes in SolidWorks and the outer 3x3, 4x4, and 5x5 boards in AutoCAD before printing. First round, the potentiometer hole came out too small — a makerspace worker walked us through drilling it open instead of reprinting. We dialed in a working tolerance so the washer fits snugly without tape or glue.

  12. March 10, 2026 PM Handoff

    M4 kickoff and the washer pivot

    Project-manager handoff to Sam for M4 and a planning session for the next stretch. Most of the discussion was how to make the box-to-box connection reliable. We settled on buying small metal washers and bonding the wire to them with copper tape — a more consistent contact surface than what we had. We also decided to support 3x3, 4x4, and 5x5 board sizes so the puzzle can scale in difficulty. Next session: fix each component box's CAD to accept the washer, and laser-cut the three board sizes.

  13. February 26, 2026 Milestone

    50% milestone — CAD lock-in, first 3D prints, wired prototype

    Two-hour session aimed at proving the prototype is at least 50% complete and actually functional. Each of us finalized our own component-box CAD against the real measurements taken earlier in the week — Sam's motor block, Minyoung's potentiometer block, and my LED block. Exported, printed, cleaned up the prints, and assembled. I drew up the flowchart for the toy's logic: a button or potentiometer press only does anything if the circuit closes, in which case the LED, motor, and buzzer all activate. Wired the components inside the box, tested end-to-end, and it worked. The prototype is fully assembled, wired, and demoable.

    Wired prototype + flowchart
  14. February 24, 2026 Ideation

    Brainstorm: locking in the approach

    Hour-long ideation meeting in the classroom. We started by listing every design idea without judging anything, then filtered against time, materials, and skill level. Came out with a finalized layout direction and a clear next step — produce a scaled sketch with component placement and measurements before our next build session.

  15. February 19, 2026 CAD

    Lock-in and start CADing against real components

    Goal of the session was to stop changing directions and commit to a final prototype concept, then start building toward it. Once aligned, we moved to the makerspace and began CAD modelling. The critical detail we'd been guessing at before: we measured the actual electronics with calipers so the CAD geometry matches reality. Sam measured the DC motor (19.7 × 15.0 mm body, 28.6 mm tall not including the 2 mm axle, 10.5 mm from the bottom to the top of the red drive section); Minyoung measured the potentiometer (6.7 mm top base, 5.9 mm knob, 16.6 mm bottom circle base, 10.7 mm bottom height). Foundation is now accurate CAD on top of real dimensions.

  16. February 12, 2026 Meeting

    Professor Keyvani approves the project

    Met with Professor Keyvani to walk through our solution. The project was approved, and we picked up useful feedback on design and user-interface considerations — especially around tailoring the experience more directly to the specific needs of the MLA students. Next priority is building a working box that proves the conductive material actually completes the circuit, in time for Friday's presentation.

    Notes from the Keyvani meeting
  17. February 11, 2026 Prototype

    First cardboard prototype in the makerspace

    Two-hour build session in the Makerspace — laser cutter, hot glue, cardboard, and X-Acto knives. Got the first physical prototype assembled. The big lesson was tolerance: cardboard thickness threw off the inner box dimensions, which pushed us toward 3D-printing the inner modules so the fits are precise and adjustable. Locked in final dimensions with tolerances and shipped a working first prototype before the 8 PM makerspace close.

    Cardboard prototype — first build
  18. February 6, 2026 Ideation

    Solution ideation and final KTDA decision

    Each team member sketched three solution concepts using the morphological chart and other ideation techniques, then we ran a KTDA to score everything against safety, electronics, age-appropriateness, problem-solving, fun, realism to build, and ease of use. The winning concept was a circuit-puzzle "power grid building" toy — students complete a circuit by snapping components into a labeled board to make an LED light up. My three concepts fed into this: a large breadboard with snap components and puzzle challenge cards, a "guess the hidden circuit" lightbulb game, and a two-player reaction time button game. The morphological chart helped us settle on the input method (push buttons), feedback (LEDs), game style (matching), and difficulty (randomized challenge cards).

    KTDA chart + ideation sketches
  19. February 2, 2026 PM Handoff

    Project manager handoff and market solution research

    Minyoung (M1 PM) handed off logistics and notes to me as the M2 project manager. The team gave PM feedback — biggest themes were staying on top of schedule, being proactive in the group chat, and finding times that work for everyone. We narrowed the STEM topic to electronics and each member found existing market solutions to use as references. My picks were the littleBits Arduino Coding Kit (Fourney & Terry, 2012) for its magnetic-snap modular design and the Squishy Circuits bioelectronic kit (Katsoulakis et al., 2025) for its use of conductive vs. insulating dough. Sam looked at aesthetic ESP32-based circuit kits, the Kids First Coding & Robotics set, and stretchable paper-based Chibi Chip circuits. Minyoung covered Snap Circuits and littleBits in more depth. The hard part was finding kits genuinely pitched at the MLA students' level.

  20. January 29, 2026 Problem Statement

    Problem statement locked in

    Two-hour session in the library seminar room to write the formal problem statement. The primary users are 5th graders at Melrose Leadership Academy in Oakland; secondary stakeholders are their teachers and parents. The core need is a hands-on STEM game that makes learning genuinely fun while still being age-appropriate, educational, and engaging — not just gamified busywork. Success looks like high student engagement, measurable improvement in curiosity and understanding, and positive feedback from teachers. Candidate solution levers we flagged: in-game incentives, friendly competition, and very tactile / hands-on play.

  21. January 28, 2026 Research

    Background research debrief

    30-minute round-robin where each member presented their literature findings. My takeaway: well-designed STEM games consistently improve engagement, motivation, and cognitive learning outcomes, and the strongest results come from interactivity, real-world context, and inclusive design — especially for younger learners. Sam flagged a critical gap in age-appropriate design: Generation Alpha students need interactive, hands-on play that goes beyond point-system gamification. Minyoung surfaced concrete game mechanics that worked in arithmetic studies — scores, trophies, timers, weekly leaderboards, audio feedback, and color-coded responses. The shared direction: hands-on, age-appropriate, learning-goal-driven.

  22. January 27, 2026 Research

    Background research — individual literature search

    Each member pulled at least five peer-reviewed sources on game-based STEM learning for elementary students, primarily from Engineering Village and Web of Science. My five focused on joyful / game-based learning outcomes, social robots advising children on group tasks, AR educational experiences tied to UN sustainability goals, serious games for children with motor dysgraphia, and narrative video games in classrooms. Together they pointed at interactivity + contextualized real-world problems + inclusive design as the recurring success factors.

  23. January 22, 2026 Decision

    Settled on a sci-fi STEM game direction

    Short follow-up meeting in the library seminar room to consolidate what we heard at MLA. Three signals stood out: 5th graders love the sci-fi genre, they love their English class because of how much freedom it gives them, and they push back hard on a heavy courseload. From those we agreed to tailor the project as a sci-fi-flavored STEM game — light enough to feel like play, but with real learning goals underneath.

  24. January 16, 2026 Empathize

    User visit at Melrose Leadership Academy

    Two-hour site visit to MLA in Oakland. We interviewed the science and English teachers about where students struggle, and talked directly with 5th graders about what they actually enjoy. From those conversations we built the Stakeholder Profile Canvas — wants (learn better, have fun, be engaged, learn in Spanish), pains (boring teachers, losing focus, hard subjects), needs (short and simple instructions, visual/interactive features), and the surrounding activities and objects in a 5th-grade classroom. This is the empathy anchor we'll keep coming back to.
